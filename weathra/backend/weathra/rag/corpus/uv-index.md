# The UV index

The UV index is a linear scale of the strength of ultraviolet radiation reaching the ground, where
one unit corresponds to 25 milliwatts per square metre of erythemally-weighted irradiance — that
is, weighted by how much each wavelength contributes to reddening skin.

## The bands

| Index | Band |
|---|---|
| 0 to 2 | Low |
| 3 to 5 | Moderate |
| 6 to 7 | High |
| 8 to 10 | Very high |
| 11 and above | Extreme |

The scale is open-ended and linear: an index of 8 is twice the intensity of 4, and burns skin in
roughly half the time.

## What drives it

- **Sun angle**, which dominates everything else. The index peaks around solar noon and is highest
  in summer and at low latitudes, because a higher sun means a shorter path through the atmosphere.
- **Cloud**, which reduces it — but far less than people expect. Thin or broken cloud may cut it
  only slightly, and scattered cloud can briefly *raise* it above the clear-sky value through
  reflection off cloud edges.
- **Altitude**, adding roughly 10% per 1000 metres.
- **Surface reflection**: fresh snow can nearly double the effective exposure, and water and sand
  add appreciably.
- **Ozone**, which absorbs UV-B and varies seasonally and geographically.

## Common misreadings

The most consequential is treating a cool day as a low-UV day. Temperature has almost nothing to do
with UV: a bright, cold, high-altitude spring day can carry a higher index than a hazy, hot
midsummer afternoon. Sunburn on a ski slope is the classic result.

The second is assuming shade removes exposure. Around half of UV at ground level is scattered from
the whole sky rather than arriving directly from the sun, so open shade still carries a substantial
fraction of the direct-sun value.

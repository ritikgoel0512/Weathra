# Wind speed, gusts, and direction

Three numbers describe wind, and each answers a different question.

## Sustained speed

The sustained (or mean) wind speed is an average over a defined period — usually ten minutes by
international convention, though some services use one minute or two minutes. Because it is an
average, it is always lower than the peaks within it. Comparing a ten-minute mean from one source
with a one-minute mean from another will show a difference that is an artefact of the averaging,
not of the weather.

## Gusts

A gust is a brief peak, conventionally a maximum sustained for a few seconds. Gusts typically run
about 1.3 to 1.5 times the mean speed over open ground, and considerably more in unstable air,
during showers, or in the lee of buildings and terrain.

Gusts are what cause damage. Structures, trees, awnings, and cyclists respond to peak force, not to
an average, so the gust figure is the one to plan around. A moderate mean speed with a very high
gust factor describes a squally, unpleasant day that a mean-speed figure alone would understate.

## Direction

Wind direction is reported as the direction the wind is coming *from*, in degrees clockwise from
true north: 270° is a westerly, blowing from west to east.

Direction cannot be averaged arithmetically. The mean of 350° and 10° is 180° by ordinary
arithmetic — the exact opposite of the correct answer, which is due north. A prevailing direction
must be computed as a vector mean, and Weathra reports it as a compass sector rather than a single
degree, because a sector is what a reader can act on and a degree implies a precision a variable
wind does not have.

## The Beaufort scale

Beaufort numbers describe wind by its observable effects — force 4 raises dust and small branches
move, force 8 breaks twigs and impedes walking. It is a description of consequences rather than a
measurement, which is why it remains useful alongside a speed in metres per second.

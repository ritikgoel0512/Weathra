# Atmospheric and surface pressure

Atmospheric pressure is the weight of the air above a point, usually reported in hectopascals
(hPa), which are numerically identical to the older millibars. Around 1013 hPa is a typical
sea-level value.

## Surface pressure versus mean sea-level pressure

Two different numbers are both called "pressure", and confusing them is the most common error.

- **Surface pressure** is what an instrument at that location actually measures. It falls with
  altitude — roughly 1 hPa per 8 metres near sea level — so a mountain station reads far lower
  than a coastal one on the same day, with no weather difference implied.
- **Mean sea-level pressure** adjusts the reading to what it would be at sea level, so that values
  from different altitudes can be compared and weather systems can be drawn on a chart.

A single pressure value is only interpretable once you know which of the two it is.

## What the trend tells you

The *change* in pressure carries more information than the value. Falling pressure means an
approaching low-pressure system, and generally more cloud, wind, and precipitation. Rising pressure
means a building high, and generally settling, clearer conditions. Rapid falls — more than a few
hPa in three hours — indicate a vigorous system and strengthening wind.

The reason is that wind flows from high to low pressure, deflected by the Earth's rotation, so a
steep pressure *gradient* between two places is what produces strong wind. A low-pressure reading
by itself does not mean wind; a tight gradient does.

## What it does not tell you

Pressure is not a proxy for temperature, and a "high" is not the same thing as a warm spell — in
winter a persistent high can bring the coldest, stillest, frostiest weather of the season.

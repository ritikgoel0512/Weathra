# Dew point

The dew point is the temperature to which air would have to be cooled, at constant pressure and
moisture content, for it to become saturated — the point at which water vapour begins to condense
into dew, mist, or fog.

## Why it is the better humidity measure

Unlike relative humidity, the dew point moves only when the actual moisture content of the air
changes. Warm the air and the dew point stays where it is; the relative humidity falls. That
stability is what makes it comparable: two places with the same dew point hold the same amount of
water vapour, whatever their temperatures are doing.

The gap between temperature and dew point — the *dew point depression* — is a direct read on how
close the air is to saturation. A small gap means damp, close conditions and a real chance of fog
overnight. A large gap means dry air.

## How it reads in practice

As a rough guide for most temperate climates, and stated as a guide rather than a rule:

- A dew point below about 10 °C feels comfortable or dry.
- Around 13 to 16 °C starts to feel noticeably humid.
- Above about 20 °C feels oppressive, because sweat evaporates poorly.

The dew point also sets a floor on how far the temperature can fall overnight: as air cools towards
its dew point, condensation releases latent heat and slows the cooling. An overnight minimum
forecast close to the evening dew point is a common and reasonable expectation.

## Relationship to other measures

Dew point, temperature, and relative humidity are three views of two independent quantities: fix
any two and the third follows. Weathra reports temperature and dew point from the provider where
they are supplied, and relative humidity alongside them; none of the three is derived from the
others inside Weathra.

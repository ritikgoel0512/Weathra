# Relative humidity

Relative humidity is the amount of water vapour in the air expressed as a percentage of the most
the air could hold at its current temperature. At 100% the air is saturated: it cannot take up more
vapour without some condensing out.

## Why it depends on temperature

Warm air can hold far more water vapour than cold air. That single fact explains most of what looks
strange about humidity readings. If a parcel of air keeps exactly the same amount of vapour but its
temperature rises, its relative humidity falls — nothing was removed, the capacity simply grew.
This is why relative humidity usually peaks in the coolest part of the night and dips in the
afternoon, often varying by tens of percentage points across a day on which the actual moisture
content barely moved.

It is also why relative humidity alone is a poor guide to how humid the air *feels*. 80% at 2 °C is
drier air, in absolute terms, than 50% at 25 °C.

## What it is useful for

Relative humidity tells you how close the air is to condensing, which is what matters for fog, dew,
and mist forming, for how quickly washing dries or paint cures, and for the risk of condensation on
cold surfaces. Sustained values above roughly 90% with light wind are the conditions fog forms in.

For "how muggy is it", dew point is the better measure, because it expresses the moisture content
itself rather than a ratio against a moving ceiling.

## Common misreadings

- **High relative humidity does not mean it is about to rain.** Rain needs lifting and cooling
  through a depth of atmosphere, not just damp air near the ground.
- **100% does not mean the air is full of water.** It means the air is saturated at that
  temperature, which at freezing point is very little water in absolute terms.
- **A falling reading through the morning is normal**, and usually reflects warming rather than
  drying.
